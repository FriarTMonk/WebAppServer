# The Parable of the Double-Minded Executive

<ParableStory>
James suited up at 6:15 AM, same as always. Forty-four years old, fourteen years as a senior logistics executive for one of Anchorage’s largest supply-chain firms. He loved the adrenaline—the closed deals, the quarterly wins, the satisfaction of keeping Alaska’s remote villages stocked through ice-road season. Work was his calling, his platform, or so he told the men’s group every other Wednesday.

But the days blurred. One priority crashed into the next: a new software rollout, an emergency vendor crisis, a last-minute investor pitch, a “quick” 7 p.m. strategy call that bled into family dinner. He told himself: *This is what success looks like. Juggling for the kingdom.*

---

Months turned into years of constant motion. The man who once opened every board meeting with prayer now barely glanced at his Bible app. Sunday worship became “when I can make it.” A big contract that required shading the truth? He justified it—“everybody does it.” The same James who posted “Love God, love people” on LinkedIn snapped at his wife over a missed soccer game and scrolled through work emails instead of praying with his kids.

His hands moved fast but his heart wandered. Missed deadlines crept in. Sleep was restless; coffee became his only constant. He felt successful at work and empty at home. The gap between the Christian he claimed to be and the man he actually was grew wider every quarter.

He knew the signs. He’d heard the pastor talk about being “double-minded” (James 1:8). But admitting the disconnect felt like career suicide. *I’m the provider. I’m the leader. I can’t slow down.*

Prayer became a checkbox. The Bible stayed on the nightstand. *God understands,* he thought. *He knows how busy life is up here.*

---

One Thursday evening after a late strategy session, James met his old mentor, Tom, for coffee at a quiet spot near the airport. Tom had discipled him years ago and still checked in. After small talk, Tom looked him square in the eye.

“James,” he said quietly, “what do you stand for? If you don’t stand for something, you will fall for anything.”

The words landed like a slap. James laughed it off at first—“Come on, Tom, I stand for the company, for my family, for faith…”—but the question echoed all the way home. That night he couldn’t sleep. The compromises, the drift, the way his life looked nothing like the convictions he professed—it all rushed in.

The next morning, bleary-eyed in the airport lounge between flights, he typed into the search bar: “Christian help when my life doesn’t match what I say I believe.” The first result: MyChristianCounselor Online. An AI counselor, available even between gates in Ted Stevens Airport.

*This is ridiculous,* he thought. But ridiculous beat silent.

He typed with thumbs still cold from the tarmac: “I say I follow Jesus but my schedule, my decisions, my temper prove something else. What now?”

The response came gentle: *"James, you’re not alone. Many faithful servants discover the painful gap between profession and practice. James 2:17—‘In the same way, faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead.’ Can you tell me more?"*

He poured it out—the chaos, the compromises, the guilt that he was modeling the opposite of what he preached, and how Tom’s words had shaken him: “If you don’t stand for something, you will fall for anything.”

*"The gap isn’t condemnation, James. It’s an invitation. God already knows the real you (Psalm 139:1-4). Let’s start with one thing today that proves your belief is real—and stand on it."*

---

He didn’t fix everything overnight. But he started with one small step: a note in his phone each morning—*What would loving God and loving people look like in today’s meetings?* Then he did one thing that proved it. A kind word to the frustrated warehouse manager. A text of encouragement to his wife. Ten minutes of Scripture before opening email. It felt awkward. But he showed up—and he stood on it.

He cared for his soul: a real lunch break instead of eating at his desk, a boundary on evening calls so he could be present at home. Small. Obedient. Firm.

---

Weeks later, harder steps. He called Tom back: “You were right. Can we talk weekly? I need someone who won’t just tell me to hustle harder.” They started Thursday check-ins. No fixing, just accountability to stand firm. Galatians 6:2 came alive—bear one another’s burdens.

He carved out one “sacred hour” each week—no laptop, no phone—just prayer, the Word, and rest. Philippians 1:6 whispered: *He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion.*

---

Months in, deeper changes emerged naturally. He spent intentional time with God—not out of duty but hunger. Confessed the double-mindedness: “I was drifting, not standing.” Repented. Reconnected to the Spirit who empowers conviction (Galatians 5:16).

He evaluated: Who was he beyond the title? Identity in Christ first, business as worship (Colossians 3:23), not his god. He even rewrote his personal mission statement around Matthew 22:37-40, shared it with his team—low-pressure, high-integrity—and stood by it.

---

Quarter four. Today.

James stepped into the 7 a.m. leadership meeting present, not frantic. The same chaotic calendar existed, but something inside him had steadied. Decisions flowed from conviction instead of pressure. Home felt warmer; he laughed with his kids instead of checking Slack. His notebook now ended with prayers of thanks—and resolve.

The demands hadn’t changed. Flights still left at 5:30 a.m. Deadlines still loomed. Vendors still pushed.

But James had changed.

He’d learned that what we truly believe is revealed by what we actually do—not by what we post or profess. Tom’s question had been the spark: “If you don’t stand for something, you will fall for anything.” Recovery wasn’t about doing more—it was about standing on the next right thing in the power of the One who promises to finish what He started.

Some days the old pull of chaos still whispered. But he remembered James 2:17—and the call to stand. He chose honesty. One obedient step. Accountability. Rest. Time with God.

And slowly, faith became alive—not from perfect performance, but from the Son who makes the double-minded whole.
</ParableStory>

<SubtleMoral>
What we truly believe is revealed not by what we say but by what we do. The gap between profession and practice isn’t a dead end—it’s a divine invitation to stand firm in conviction: name the drift honestly, remember God’s grace first, take one obedient step today (and stand on it), lean on community, rest in His presence, and fix your eyes on the finish line—until our daily rhythms reflect the greatest commandments and our lives become a living testimony that faith without works is dead.
</SubtleMoral>

<ScriptureTieIn verse="James 2:17" version="NIV">
"In the same way, faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead."

Jesus doesn’t condemn the double-minded—He invites them to stand. The disconnect James felt is the same one many believers experience when self-reliance and busyness replace walking by the Spirit (Galatians 5:16). His story echoes the blog’s clear truth: God already knows us completely (Psalm 139:1-4), Jesus laid His life down while we were still sinners (Romans 5:8), and the Holy Spirit gives us real power to resist the flesh and live what we profess.

Alignment isn’t gritting through more hustle—it’s coming to Him and standing firm. Start small: name the gap honestly (Psalm 139), remember God’s love first (1 John 4:19), do one thing today that proves your belief is real (encouragement, kindness, prayer, Scripture), lean on community (Galatians 6:2), rest in His presence, and fix your eyes on the finish line (Philippians 3:12-14). God promises He will complete the good work He began (Philippians 1:6).

When we realign—identity first in Christ, work as worship rather than idol—the burden lightens. Chaos gives way to peace, not because the calendar empties, but because we stop drifting and stand with the Spirit who leads us into the life we say we believe.

Even a whispered “Lord, help me stand for what I say I believe—one step at a time” is the beginning of wholeness.
</ScriptureTieIn>
